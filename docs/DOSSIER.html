01  /  The problem

A farmer's crops and animals are one business. Every app treats them as two.

Problem Statement 1 asks for four things in one platform: crop disease identification, livestock monitoring, historical farm records, and actionable advisory services, with an interface that works in a rural environment.

Read closely, the hard word in that sentence is unified. Each of the four pieces already exists somewhere. Plantix diagnoses crops. Bharat Pashudhan has tagged 29.6 of India's 30.5 crore bovines. Kisan e-Mitra answers scheme questions by voice in eleven languages. Agmarknet publishes mandi prices. None of them holds two of those facts at the same time, and so none of them can produce a sentence that requires both.

That matters because a smallholding is not four separate businesses. The maize stover is the cattle fodder. The money for the vaccine comes from the wheat sale. A cow that stops eating in the same week the crop is sprayed is not a coincidence a crop app can notice.

What the field actually tells us

Three findings shaped every decision in this build, and each one points away from the obvious answer.

Accuracy is not the bottleneck. Trust is. Disease-detection apps with 95% lab accuracy have shown under 10% field adoption. Ama Krushi reached 7 million farmers in Odisha with plain voice calls, no computer vision at all, and returned $9 to $15 of farmer benefit for every $1 spent.

The consequence is uncomfortable for a hackathon: an 85%-accurate system that admits doubt beats a 95%-accurate one that never does. A confident wrong diagnosis costs a farmer a spray, a season, and their willingness to open the app again.

Apps alone do not reach the farmers who need them most. Kisan Suvidha has roughly 3 lakh users; mKisan's SMS service has 5 crore registrations. 51.6% of rural women aged 15 and over do not own a mobile phone at all, in a workforce that is 64.3% women.

So voice is the primary interface, not an accessibility afterthought, and every dead end in the app terminates at a human being with a phone number, not at an error screen.

Nobody has unified crop and livestock. This is the gap, and it is a structural one, not a feature one.
02  /  The idea

One record. One table. Everything else falls out of that.

HAL keeps a farmer's crops and animals in a single append-only event log, so the advice they receive can see both halves of their farm at once.

This sounds like a database detail. It is the entire product. Every differentiating feature in this document is a query over that one table, and no competitor can add these features without rebuilding their data model from the ground up.

The Marathi translation is complete but has not been read by a native speaker. Neither is hidden; both are tracked as blocking items for field deployment rather than for the demo.

04  /  Features

What each part does, and what it changes for the person holding the phone.

Photograph a leaf, name the disease

Live

The farmer points the camera at a leaf. A MobileNetV3 classifier inside the APK returns a disease, a severity, and a confidence. If a plot is selected, the model is restricted to that crop's diseases before it decides, which removes roughly a third of the ways it can be wrong.

What changes: a diagnosis in about two seconds, in the field, with no signal and no data charge. The alternative today is a photo sent to a WhatsApp group and an answer that may never come.

The app refuses to guess

Live

Every diagnosis passes a calibrated confidence gate with three outcomes. This is the single most important design decision in the product, and it is the one most likely to be mistaken for a weakness.

Above 0.85  ·  certain
97.9%

Names the disease, shows the dose.

Right 97.9% of the times it speaks. Includes chemical, quantity per litre, timing, and the rupee cost against the rupee loss avoided.

0.60 to 0.85  ·  checking
24.0%

Names it, withholds the dose.

Routed to a KVK expert with a case number. The farmer sees what it might be, and that a human is looking, but never a dose nobody has confirmed.

Below 0.60  ·  will not guess
30.8%

Says nothing at all.

No disease name, not even a suggestion. A real phone number instead: Kisan Call Centre 1800-180-1551, or 1962 for animals.

What changes: the farmer can believe the app. On the hardest photographs we test against, it volunteers an answer on one image in twenty-six, and is right on 92% of those. It knows what it does not know, and that is measurable, not a claim.

Vaccination calendar that arrives before the date

Live

Enter an animal once. The app derives the entire schedule from its species and age, then schedules Android alarms that fire whether or not the app is open, whether or not there is signal. Recorded doses and missing records are treated as different things: a dose that expired is a fact, a row that was never written is only an inference, and the app says so in different words.

What changes: the vaccine is already free and the government already administers it. The only thing standing between the farmer and it is knowing the date. This is the highest impact-to-effort feature in the entire application, and it is date arithmetic.

Two questions to a diagnosis, for an animal

Live

A rule tree written with veterinary triage logic, not a model. “Can she stand?” “Has she calved in the last three days?” Two taps to milk fever, with the caveat “we are not certain” kept on screen, an urgent instruction, and a call button.

What changes: India has roughly 41,000 veterinarians for about 53.58 crore livestock. Hours matter in hypocalcaemia. The app cannot be the vet; it can be the thing that makes the farmer call one tonight instead of tomorrow.

One ledger for the whole farm

Live

Sowing, irrigation, spray, harvest, expense, vaccination, calving, disease scan: all of it in one feed, split into what is coming, what is today, and what has passed. Nothing here is typed twice; every row is a byproduct of something the farmer already did in the app.

What changes: the farmer ends a season with a record they never sat down to write. That record is what makes next season's advice specific instead of generic.

Ask anything, out loud

Live

The farmer speaks or types a question. Before answering, the app sends its own timeline up with the question, so the model is answering about this farm and not about farming. Retrieval runs over a vetted corpus of ICAR and DAHD documents; anything outside it escalates to the Kisan Call Centre rather than being invented.

What changes: ask “what should I do now?” with no crop and no disease named, and get an answer about your maize and your cow in the same paragraph, citing the institution it came from.

Everything important works with the network off

Live

Diagnosis, symptom triage, the ledger and the reminders are all local. Writes land in the phone's database first and always. When a connection returns, an append-only queue drains by itself; because events cannot conflict, there is no sync engine to go wrong.

What changes: the app is usable standing in a field, which is where the leaf is.

The farmer's own language, including the words they actually use

Live

Hindi, English and Marathi, complete and validated for parity on every build. The Marathi is deliberately the farmer's vocabulary rather than the dictionary's: शेत not खेत, जनावर not पशु, लाळ्या खुरकूत for foot and mouth disease. Languages whose translations do not exist are shown as जल्द आएगी rather than silently rendering Hindi.

What changes: the app speaks the farmer's language before it asks them for anything. That is the cheapest trust signal available and it is on the first screen.

Village outbreak view

Built, needs density

Confirmed diagnoses aggregate by pincode and week. When several farmers in one area report the same disease, that is a signal worth pushing back out.

What changes: one farmer's scan becomes a neighbour's early warning. This is a GROUP BY, and it only works because the diagnoses are honest enough to aggregate.

Weather-triggered advice, not a weekly newsletter

Live

The app speaks when something crosses a threshold. Rain tomorrow and an unsprayed infection today produces one alert; nothing happening produces silence.

What changes: an empty home screen is a feature. Alert fatigue is what kills every generic SMS advisory service ever run.

05  /  Under the hood

Local-first by construction, not by configuration.

The phone is the source of truth. The server holds a synced copy, the retrieval corpus, and the language model call. If the server disappears, the farmer loses the advisory and keeps everything else.

Decisions that look small and are not

  • Area is stored in hectares, always. A bigha is not a fixed quantity in India. Hard-coding one conversion would make every dose and every cost silently wrong by up to three times, with no error anywhere.
  • Nothing the app can derive is asked of the farmer. Soil type comes from GPS, not from a dropdown.
  • No daily logging of anything. A record the farmer has to maintain is a record that stops after a week. Every row is a byproduct of an action they already took.
  • Confidence thresholds live in one file. Recalibrating the model must never require touching a screen.
  • No sync engine. Append-only events cannot conflict, so a flag and a batch POST is the entire feature.
06  /  The model

Trained on 41,933 images, and measured two different ways on purpose.

One model is trained. Everything else in the application is rules, date arithmetic, SQL, or a retrieval call, because those are more reliable and a hackathon has finite hours.

How it was trained

  1. Twelve datasets merged into one taxonomy of 30 classes. Source folders that do not map are dropped and printed, never guessed. Thirty-four folder names are refused by name with a written reason, because each one looks mappable and is not.
  2. Content-hash deduplication with field images ranked first. Public datasets quietly repackage each other; without this, held-out photographs end up in training.
  3. MobileNetV3-Small, ImageNet weights. Six epochs training the head, then six more with the top third of the backbone unfrozen at a tenth of the learning rate.
  4. Augmentation aimed at one specific failure. Random erasing, grayscale, saturation and hue, on top of the usual geometry. The model had learned that a plain background means it is safe to be confident, because almost every lab image is a detached leaf on a bench.
  5. Temperature scaling fitted on held-out field images, never on the validation split. Calibrating on studio imagery produces a gate tuned for photographs the app will never see.
  6. Exported to float16 TFLite. INT8 was built, measured, and rejected: it changed 7 of 30 predictions. float16 agrees with the float model exactly and still fits in 1.98 MB.

Two field numbers, and why both are printed

A donated field dataset is usually one team, one camera and a handful of sessions. Holding images out of it does not hold out much, because the test photograph was taken minutes later in the same row. So the model is scored twice.

Quote 46.9%. PlantDoc is scraped from across the open web, shares no collection with anything in training, and is the closest thing available to a stranger's phone. Anyone who has trained one of these models will ask precisely this question, and having the breakdown ready is worth more than the larger number.

The two cotton rows show why. Cotton originally came from a single institute's field and scored 97.9% on images held out of it. Adding a second, unrelated cotton survey dropped that row to 93.0% and put the new one at 87.6%. The number went down, and that is what a real measurement looks like.

Read the right-hand column

On PlantDoc the model scores 46.9% and volunteers an answer on one photograph in twenty-six, and is right on 92% of those. On ordinary field photographs it answers 60 to 70% of the time and is right essentially always. The gate is not a safety net bolted on afterwards; it is the product, and the numbers show it doing exactly what it was designed to do.

v1 is the cautionary tale: it answered confidently on a third of field photographs and was wrong 71% of those times. v4 answers four and a half times as often as v3 and is wrong once in forty-eight instead of once in six. Validation accuracy across all four builds moved from 93.2% to 93.1%, which is to say it never moved at all. Everything that mattered happened in the field column, and none of it was ever visible from validation.

07  /  Why it works

Every mechanism here has already worked in the field. None of them together.

This is not a bet on an untested idea. It is a bet that four separately proven interventions are worth more in one record than in four apps.

No existing platform can produce this sentence, because none of them holds both facts. “Spray the maize before tomorrow's rain, and Gauri's foot-and-mouth vaccine was due 78 days ago.”

The adoption argument, in one line

Every proven intervention above shares one property: the farmer believed it. Nuru's 14.1% adoption and Ama Krushi's 7 million users are the same lesson read from both ends. HAL spends its engineering on the believing, not on the last few points of accuracy, and the confidence gate is where that money went.

08  /  Roadmap

Where it is, and what each next stage unlocks.

Nowv4 · shipped

A working application with an honest model

  • 30 classes, 6 crops, on-device, 1.98 MB, tier-1 precision 97.9%
  • Vaccination calendar, symptom triage, merged ledger, cross-domain advisory
  • Hindi, English, Marathi; full offline round trip verified on a device
  • Backend deployed; the phone repairs the server's record by itself after a wipe
NextWeeks 1–4

Make it safe to put in a real farmer's hands

  • KVK sign-off on doses, veterinary triage, and state land-unit conversions. Every one of these files already prints a warning until it is signed
  • Native review of the Marathi, then Punjabi, Bengali and Telugu, which need their own script fonts bundled
  • Mandi price feed showing net realisable price, the headline minus transport, which is the number the farmer actually receives
  • Field pilot with one KVK: 30 farmers, one district, real photographs
ThenMonths 2–6

The data flywheel starts turning

  • Expert-confirmed tier-2 cases become labelled Indian field training data. Every escalation the app makes improves the model that caused it
  • Independent accuracy rises because the training distribution finally matches the phones in use, not because the backbone got bigger
  • Village outbreak map goes live once diagnosis density supports it
  • In-app community Q&A: answered threads become searchable, so one question is answered once instead of fifty times
  • VLAE network — the village-level agri entrepreneur as the human channel for farmers the app cannot reach directly
LaterMonths 6–18

From advisory to farm operating system

  • Year-on-year comparison and a farm income statement, both already latent in the ledger and needing only a second season of data
  • Bhashini integration for genuinely multilingual voice across 22 languages
  • Government scheme matching against the farm's own record, so eligibility is computed rather than searched
  • Insurance claim assistance under PMFBY, where the timeline is the evidence
  • Warehouse receipt financing and KCC pre-approval, where the ledger becomes a credit history for a farmer who has never had one
  • Satellite plot health, drone services, and export linkage as partnerships rather than in-house builds

Deliberately not on this roadmap

Muzzle biometric identification was cut after being costed: a farmer with three animals already knows which cow is which, and muzzle ID solves an institutional traceability problem, not a marginal farmer's problem. Sensors and collars are rejected on the same grounds at ₹10,000–15,000 per animal for hardware designed for 500-cow dairies. Rejecting both is part of the pitch, not a gap in it.

Advisory corpus

The retrieval corpus is drawn from ICAR Package of Practices, DAHD and NDDB veterinary guidance, and the National Animal Disease Control Programme schedules. Every answer the app gives names the document it came from; anything outside the corpus escalates to the Kisan Call Centre rather than being generated.

What we refused to cite

  • A widely repeated “98.2% on a 10,516-image dataset from 18 farms” figure for lumpy skin disease. We could not verify it; the 98.2% figures we could trace belong to skin-cancer and lung-cancer ensemble papers.
  • Any unit economics — customer acquisition cost, lifetime value, break-even. We have no basis for them, so they do not appear.
  • “Farmers get 45% of the mandi price.” The RBI figures above are the verifiable version and we use those instead.
